Recently, during my Introduction to Financial Economics course, my instructor was teaching us about government securities (G-secs).

The assumption I walked in with

He asked us what risks could be associated with a G-sec. Naturally, having studied the subject on my own, my first instinct was to think: There is no risk, right? After all, a sovereign government issuing debt in its own currency should, in principle, be able to repay it. At the very least, I assumed, we would get back the principal amount invested. The possibility of interest rates rising and the market value of our bonds falling was a separate matter altogether.

Our instructor then introduced us to the case of Zimbabwe and the hyperinflation it experienced.

I had, of course, heard of hyperinflation. I had seen the post-World War I images of Germans carrying carts full of banknotes just to buy a loaf of bread. However, I had always assumed that hyperinflation was largely a historical phenomenon, rather than a problem that modern sovereign governments could still face.

Turns out, that assumption was far from correct. Zimbabwe pushed the limits of monetary instability to an extraordinary extent.

The printing press is not a free pass

When a government faces a shortage of funds, one option available to it is to finance its spending by creating more money. Countries with their own currencies and monetary institutions may have this option, although the process is not as simple as turning on the printing press. Creating money to finance government spending can have serious consequences, particularly when it is not supported by the economy’s productive capacity.

Countries that use a shared currency, such as the euro, face a different constraint. Individual eurozone members cannot independently instruct their central banks to print more euros to finance their spending. Can you imagine Greece simply asking the European Union to print more money to bail it out? That is not how the system works, although financial assistance can be provided through other mechanisms.

Zimbabwe: when the numbers stop feeling real

Zimbabwe, however, resorted to monetary expansion amid severe fiscal and economic difficulties. The result was hyperinflation on an almost unimaginable scale. By November 2008, an estimate by economists Steve Hanke and Alex Kwok put Zimbabwe’s annual inflation rate at 89.7 sextillion per cent—an almost incomprehensible figure, equivalent to 89.7 × 1021 per cent. Prices were reportedly doubling approximately every 24.7 hours.

A crisis of this magnitude can destroy a currency’s purchasing power, leaving people with banknotes that may have enormous face values but very little real value.

Repaid, but at what value?

And this brings me back to the original question:

If a sovereign government can create more of its own currency, does that make its debt risk-free?

Clearly, the answer is not that simple. A government might be able to meet its nominal repayment obligations while inflation simultaneously erodes the purchasing power of the money investors receive. The principal may be repaid in full in numerical terms, yet be worth considerably less in real terms.

So, should a sovereign government print more money simply because it can? Or is the ability to create money itself a source of risk that investors in government securities need to understand?
